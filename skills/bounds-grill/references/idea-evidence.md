# Idea evidence

Treat an idea as a ladder of assumptions. A higher rung is worth discussing only once the rungs beneath it have evidence or a recorded waiver.

## Assumption ladder

Each rung states what must be true.

1. **Problem.** A specific kind of person has this problem now, and it costs them time, money, or risk.
2. **Switch.** They would change what they do. The push of their current situation and the pull of the new solution must outweigh their anxiety about switching and the habits or past investment that hold them in place. The real competitor is often a workaround or doing nothing.
3. **Use.** They can find, understand, and adopt it, and the first session works without help.
4. **Build.** It can be built and kept running with the skills, time, and technology available.
5. **Sustain.** It works for everything else it touches: cost, pricing, distribution, support, legal or policy limits, and the maker's own time.

Rungs 1 and 2 are the basic assumption. On a personal or exploratory project the user may waive them; record the waiver, and rung 5 may shrink to whether it is worth the maker's time. Once they have evidence or a waiver, a decision to build can continue in plan mode.

## Asking for evidence

Ask about specific past events, not opinions or hypotheticals.

- **Ask:** when did this last happen, what did they do about it, what did it cost, and what have they already tried or paid for.
- **Avoid:** "Would you use…", "Do you think people…", "How much would you pay…".
- **Not evidence:** compliments, generic claims ("everyone hates this"), promises about the future ("I'd definitely buy it"), and enthusiasm, including the user's own. Record these as opinions.
- **Evidence:** specific past events, money or time already spent, workarounds people built, commitments that cost something (a deposit, a booked session, an introduction), and observed use.

The user's own past experience is one data point; ask how they know others share it. The recommendation for these questions describes a useful answer, such as "the last occasion, what you did instead, and what it cost", and never fills it in.

## Shaping the experience

Once rungs 1 and 2 hold or are waived, question each feature against the problem. Ask for the smallest version that solves it, how the first session goes, and what could be prototyped cheaply first. Prefer a few polished features to many rough ones, and count developers as users when the idea is a library or tool.

## Pre-mortem

At the same point, ask the user to imagine the idea has failed some time from now and to name the likeliest reasons. Add your own; each becomes an assumption on the ladder.

## Rank the assumptions

Rate each assumption on two scales:

- **Impact:** does the idea fail if this is wrong?
- **Evidence:** none, opinion, past behavior, or a commitment that cost something.

The riskiest combine high impact with weak evidence. Break ties by rung, lowest first.

## Choose the first test

Pick the cheapest test that could show the riskiest assumption is wrong. Agree on pass and fail criteria before it runs, so the result can't be reinterpreted afterward; the user sets any threshold. Typical shapes:

- **Problem:** conversations about past specifics with people who match the target, or watching their current workaround.
- **Switch:** an offer that asks for a real commitment, such as a sign-up, deposit, or time, rather than an opinion.
- **Use:** a throwaway prototype, or a version run by hand behind the scenes, watched while someone attempts a real task.
- **Build:** a short spike on the riskiest technical piece.
- **Sustain:** a cost estimate, or a pricing conversation that ends in a commitment.

## Close

- The idea in one sentence: who has the problem, what it is, and what changes for them.
- Decisions made, each with its reason.
- Assumptions ranked by risk, each with its evidence level and source.
- Waivers the user chose, and why.
- The first test: assumption, method, pass and fail criteria, cost, and whether each result means stop, change, or continue.
- Why the session stopped.

The next step is usually running that test.
